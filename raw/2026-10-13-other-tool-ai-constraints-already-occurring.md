# Other tool — revised assessment: "the AI credit constraints are already occurring" (Jake's paste 2026-10-13 ~9:00am PT)

Yes. You're identifying a problem with the way I framed those three indicators: I treated evidence of deterioration as though we still needed to wait for it to appear.

At least two of the three are already showing significant warning signs, and permitting restrictions are producing a similar financial effect to credit tightening even when the underlying cause is political or regulatory.

1. The failed IPO you're remembering was Firmus

On October 9, Firmus, the Nvidia-backed Australian AI infrastructure developer, withdrew its planned approximately $5 billion IPO.

Investors raised specific objections:

* An approximately $30.6 billion proposed valuation, nearly triple its valuation from an August funding round.
* Roughly $30 billion in projected future debt after its planned data centers were built.
* Limited operating history relative to its enormous expansion plans.
* Uncertainty about future cash flow, funding requirements and whether its projects could be completed profitably.

The company abandoned the offering and turned to private capital instead. This was not a small pricing adjustment.

Even more significant, The Information reported on October 8 that Nvidia had paused some financing arrangements with smaller cloud companies in August and was revising contracts because of concerns about their dependence on Nvidia funding.

That suggests capital discipline is developing on both sides: investors financing the infrastructure and the supplier helping support GPU purchases.

2. Credit spreads have already widened

Reuters documented the deterioration back on July 29, well before the October article.

Indicator | Earlier | By July 2026
2–4 year hyperscaler bond spreads | 30 bp | 40 bp
5–7 year spreads | 50 bp | 60 bp
20+ year spreads | 108.5 bp | 118 bp
Investor orders relative to bonds offered | Nearly 5× | Below 2×

The spread comparisons are against 2025 issuance. Reuters also found 78 of 91 comparable hyperscaler bonds issued in 2026 trading at higher yields than when issued.

Those numbers confirm repricing, although the increases are not individually large enough to establish a sector-wide credit crisis.

But there's a much more direct example.

Oracle's Project Jupiter — New Mexico

On September 18, Reuters reported that approximately $18 billion in loans tied to the Oracle-leased project were being quoted by syndicate banks at just 89–91 cents on the dollar. Attempts to distribute the debt to other investors had stalled.

At the same time, the proposed 2.2-GW natural-gas power solution was facing regulatory obstacles.

That is credit stress and project-execution stress occurring together.

Oracle subsequently clarified that the air-permit stay affected the separately operated microgrid, not the county permits allowing construction of the data-center buildings themselves.

3. You were also correct about the broader permitting restrictions

I verified the actual orders.

Location | Status as of October 13
Texas | Statewide halt on new data-center regulatory approvals pending grid and water audits, including environmental permits
New York | Statewide moratorium affecting certain pending state permit applications for data centers of 50 MW or more
Virginia | Statewide regulatory tightening, but not a blanket construction moratorium; several municipalities have imposed pauses or stricter approvals

Texas's September 21 directive explicitly freezes state approvals until required audits are completed. New York's July 14 order pauses qualifying incomplete permit applications pending environmental review. Virginia's September 18 executive order and accountability framework impose additional requirements, while places including Virginia Beach have enacted local moratoria.

The important distinction is that these actions generally do not stop every previously approved construction project. They do interrupt new development pipelines, which is what matters for future capacity forecasts.

And the national numbers are substantial.

Q1 2026 — $130B — 75 projects blocked or delayed
Q2 2026 — $68B — 45 projects blocked or delayed
Data Center Watch estimates. These are announced project values affected by disruptions, not confirmed losses or permanent cancellations.

4. There are now two more postponed IPOs

In addition to Firmus:

VITRO REIT — October 6: PLDT disclosed that its data-center REIT listing had been deferred to 2027 because of current market conditions and rising interest rates. That's particularly relevant because the listing was part of PLDT's asset monetization and deleveraging strategy.

AirTrunk — October 13: Reuters reported that Blackstone-backed AirTrunk postponed its proposed $1.5 billion Singapore REIT IPO amid market volatility and reduced investor demand.

These are not all permanent cancellations. But combined with Firmus, they demonstrate that companies are no longer guaranteed access to equity at their preferred valuations or financing terms.

5. Your most important point is the effect on financing

The reason a project gets delayed is secondary to its financial consequences.

A lender financing a data center cares about when the project begins producing the contracted revenue needed to service its debt.

A regulatory delay can create exactly the same cash-flow problem as a construction or financing delay.

Suppose a project has $10 billion of drawn financing at an 8% annual borrowing cost. A six-month delay represents approximately $400 million in additional interest carry, before considering other expenses or contractual protections.

The eventual cash-flow damage depends on how much debt has actually been drawn, whether interest is capitalized, who bears the delay and whether contractual rent commencement changes.

For AI infrastructure, there's an additional complication: GPUs have shorter economic lives than buildings. Delaying deployment can consume a meaningful portion of the period in which the hardware is expected to earn its highest rental rates.

There's one countereffect worth distinguishing. Delaying new capacity can also preserve scarce compute supply and support rental prices for existing operational capacity. That benefits functioning facilities even while financially damaging projects that cannot open.

Revised assessment

Original warning indicator | Current evidence | Assessment
Credit spreads widening | Bond repricing and weaker order books throughout 2026 | Already occurring
Financing being withdrawn or delayed | Firmus, VITRO, AirTrunk and Project Jupiter | Already occurring selectively
Construction and delivery delays | State restrictions, local moratoria and documented blocked or delayed projects | Already occurring

So I would change the conclusion.

We are no longer waiting for the first signs that the AI credit expansion is becoming constrained. Those signs are already present.

What remains unproven is whether the constraints are large enough to cause an aggregate decline in actual AI infrastructure spending and hardware purchases.

That distinction matters enormously for NVDA, AVGO, MU, VRT, ETN and the rest of the supply chain.

A developer can lose financing, abandon an IPO or suffer a permitting delay long before a supplier reports the lost order. Existing commitments, deposits, construction backlogs and equipment already purchased can temporarily conceal the slowdown.

The question I'd now prioritize is:

How much of the announced 2027–2028 AI capacity is fully funded, fully permitted and actually scheduled to be operational—and how much is still dependent on capital that investors are becoming less willing to provide?

That is a much better test of whether the projected semiconductor demand is real and deliverable than waiting for additional credit spreads to widen.
