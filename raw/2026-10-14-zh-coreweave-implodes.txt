Sure enough, the same equity guys who were pumping the name every day for months even as its credit spread were blowing out, are now turning against it, and on Wednesday, D.A. Davidson analyst Gil Luria, who has correctly referred to CoreWeave as “the subprime tranche” of AI (although we would say Oracle is just the same, and far more important) warned on a podcast that the "equity value is going to zero" if the company becomes unable to pay its expensive debt obligations. Luria's radar went off when he realized that CoreWeave is borrowing money at around 9%, but it is earning returns of just 1% on its investments. Even the 4% to 5% he said the company is targeting would fall short of its borrowing costs. He compared it to taking out a loan at 9% to buy Treasury bonds yielding 5%, a strategy he argued would destroy value. CoreWeave - - considered the original “neocloud”, a batch of cloud-computing providers many of whom were formerly bitcoin miners, now focused on the artificial-intelligence market - reported $640 million in second-quarter net interest expense, more than double the $267 million recorded a year earlier, alongside a $626 million net loss. CreditSights analyst Jordan Chalfin told the Financial Times that CoreWeave had been “very creative” with its financing, but investors were worried about its long data-center leases and shorter customer contracts. “If there’s an overbuild, why would [customers] renew their contract with CoreWeave?” he asked. Helpfully, Bloomberg chimes in that in a severe restructuring, creditors would have priority over shareholders.Yes, it's called a Chapter 11 bankruptcy. That said, clearly Gil doesn't think that happens soon: he still has a Neutral rating with a $100 price targe on the stock: about 50% higher than where it is trading today. In fact, of the 49 analysts covering CRWV, just 4 have a Sell/Reduce. CRWV Co-founder Brannin McBee offered a sharply different view to the FT: “I don’t look at that as scary debt at all,” he said, arguing that borrowing allows CoreWeave to expand faster. (Narrator: the company's debt is, in fact, debt ). The company is seeking cheaper financing from insurers to continue building AI infrastructure. Its combined debt and lease obligations stood at approximately $52 billion at the end of June. Visible Alpha consensus forecasts cited by the FT project $71 billion in cumulative negative free cash flow between 2026 and 2028. The problem is that with the unsecured now trading above 15%, he won't be able to find anything cheaper than its current capital structure. Unless, of course, the company files and there is a debt for equity exchange. Then yes - the new debt of the reorganized company would be far cheaper :) But McBee argued the spending reflects expansion rather than weakness in the existing business. “If we stop building today, [we’d be] immensely cash-flow positive,” he said, unintentionally exposing the biggest weakness of the entire AI bubble: the moment one stops "building", it's all over, and so instead of doing that, the company would rather to just layer itself with as much debt as it possibly can to delay the inevitable day when it does, in fact, stop building. Judging by the stock price, that day is coming fast, and as an aside, on Polymarket, traders are now assigning a 17% probability to CoreWeave announcing bankruptcy by December 2027, with approximately $50,600 traded. The odds rise to 27% by 2029. Not everyone is waiting to find out if the skeptics are right: three of CoreWeave’s co-founders have sold about $2bn of shares this year, according to S&P Capital IQ. McBee said the founders were diversifying their personal wealth, but that most of it was still in CoreWeave shares. “We could go off to a ranch and retire,” he said. “But you’re watching us show up every single day and put our time into this business.” For now... yes, because the bond market is willing to let you show up every single day.
