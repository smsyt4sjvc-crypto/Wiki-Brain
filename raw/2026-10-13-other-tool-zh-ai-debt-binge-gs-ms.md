AI debt binge: Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley

ZeroHedge — October 6, 2026

The central finding: AI financing is beginning to tighten, but not equally across the industry. The largest hyperscalers can still borrow substantial amounts. Smaller, highly leveraged AI infrastructure companies are encountering higher borrowing costs and increasingly selective lenders.

I located the article, but most of it is behind ZeroHedge Premium. I cross-checked its claims against Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and other financial reporting.

1. The debt numbers are enormous

Measure | Reported amount
AI-related debt issued in 2026, Goldman estimate | $489B
Morgan Stanley full-year 2026 projection | ~$570B
Goldman 2027 hyperscaler bond supply projection | ~$420B
Goldman 2027 additional data-center/project financing projection | ~$300B
Reported future lease and purchase commitments | ~$3T

These are different categories and time periods. They cannot simply be added together. In particular, the $3 trillion includes future contractual commitments, not necessarily money already borrowed.

Goldman's August research anticipated debt financing approximately 33% of hyperscaler capital expenditure in 2026, increasing to 35% in 2027.

This makes lenders increasingly important to the pace of AI infrastructure expansion.

2. Where the cracks are appearing

Goldman reported several signs that investors are becoming less willing to absorb unlimited AI debt:

* Bond order books fell from nearly 5 times the amount offered in February to less than 2 times in July.
* Median new-issue concessions rose from 2.25 basis points in 2025 to 12 in 2026.
* By late July, 78 of 91 hyperscaler bonds issued during 2026 were trading at wider spreads than their original pricing.
* Goldman Sachs Asset Management was underweight hyperscaler bonds ahead of the projected 2027 issuance wave.

This is particularly important because Treasury yields and corporate credit spreads can rise together. A borrower then faces higher base interest rates and a larger risk premium.

Morgan Stanley distinguishes two markets: highly rated hyperscalers with strong operating cash flow, and leveraged developers whose financing depends on projects being completed, powered and leased. The latter are much more sensitive to borrowing costs.

3. Something significant happened after the article

On October 10, the Financial Times reported new Morgan Stanley data showing a sharp fall in AI-related debt issuance.

Global AI-related debt issuance

That represents an approximately 80% decline from June's peak. U.S. investment-grade AI bond issuance also stopped in September.

Important qualification: Morgan Stanley attributed much of the slowdown to companies having borrowed heavily earlier in the year. Falling issuance alone does not demonstrate a credit freeze.

4. What this means for AI stocks

The most vulnerable part of the chain is not necessarily Nvidia or Microsoft. It's the companies financing the infrastructure that buys their equipment.

Exposure | Primary concern
CRWV and other neoclouds | Borrowing costs, GPU collateral values, utilization
ORCL | Capital commitments, project financing, free cash flow
NVDA, AVGO, MU | Whether financed orders translate into sustained physical unit demand
VRT, ETN, GEV | Construction schedules, power connections, delayed deployments
MSFT, GOOGL, AMZN, META | Returns on capex and how quickly cash flow catches up

These are risk channels to investigate, not assertions that every company is currently experiencing financial distress.

Connection to your semiconductor-volume thesis

Your concern about revenue growing faster than semiconductor unit volumes becomes more important when debt finances incremental chip purchases.

The possible sequence is:

Credit becomes more expensive
Developers delay new capacity
GPU orders and deliveries slow
Chip pricing faces pressure
Revenue growth potentially slows sharply

That is a plausible downside scenario, not an established outcome. Better utilization, profitable AI applications and strong hyperscaler cash flow could interrupt the sequence.

5. So when does the credit party actually end?

My assessment is that credit is already being rationed at the weaker end of the AI infrastructure market, but a system-wide funding shutdown is not established.

Three indicators would tell us whether selective tightening is turning into a genuine capex contraction: widening spreads accompanied by failed financing deals, funded construction projects being delayed or canceled, and slower hardware orders despite previously announced capacity commitments.

Goldman also has a substantial counterargument: its August analysis estimated approximately $2 trillion of additional borrowing capacity before the hyperscalers reached certain investment-grade leverage assumptions. That is theoretical capacity, not a guarantee of investor demand.

There is fresh institutional concern as well. On October 13, the IMF identified an AI-related market correction as one of the major risks to global financial stability if investment or profit expectations disappoint.

The distinction that matters: demand for AI computing could remain strong while the businesses financing additional computing capacity struggle. That disconnect could first appear in credit spreads, equipment financing and project timelines—before it appears in semiconductor earnings.
