The Vercel split is becoming extreme

At the lab level, the current Sep. 6 endpoint gives:

DeepSeek: 41.3% tokens → 4.7% dollars
Anthropic: 11.5% tokens → 54.8% dollars
OpenAI: 11.3% tokens → 21.5% dollars. 

A useful derived metric is spend share ÷ token share. It is not a literal API price, because workload/input-output mix differs, but it measures the economic density of each lab’s traffic relative to the gateway average:

DeepSeek ≈ 0.11× average spend/token
OpenAI ≈ 1.90×
Anthropic ≈ 4.77×

On that share-ratio basis, Anthropic’s Vercel traffic currently carries roughly 42× DeepSeek’s spend intensity per token.

That is probably the cleanest empirical picture yet of the emerging market structure:

Commodity intelligence is winning volume.
Premium intelligence is still winning dollars.

And open-weight penetration is not slowing. Vercel’s CEO called 62% open-weight token share on Aug. 22 a record; the live Sep. 6 reading is now 65.3%.

The live September data now shows open-weight share at 65.3%, vastly beyond July’s 36%. We cannot directly translate the daily endpoint into August’s eventual monthly average, but directionally the cheap-model substitution appears to have accelerated, not reversed.

Model-margin read

There is now very strong evidence of price-performance compression, but still not enough evidence to prove gross-margin compression at OpenAI, Anthropic or DeepSeek because they do not disclose sufficiently detailed inference COGS.

The compression evidence strengthened this week: Anthropic cut Fable cache-read cost 75%; Google shipped a better Flash generation at the same token price; Google’s video routing can reduce tokens up to 88%; and open-weight models have reached 65.3% of Vercel tokens. 

But the other side also strengthened: OpenAI introduced Astra at $10/$50, 2.5× GPT-5.6 Sol, while Anthropic introduced Fable 5.1 at the same $10/$50 frontier price point. Premium capability is therefore not racing toward zero. 

The market increasingly looks like:

Cheap/open tier → brutal token commoditization and enormous volume.

Frontier tier → premium pricing survives where capability produces sufficient economic value.

That is much closer to the cell-phone-minutes/barbell thesis than to a uniform collapse in all model pricing.

MODEL COST/TOKEN ↓ structurally

The key distinction is that efficiency is compressing the economics of the model unit while expanding the physical infrastructure required to supply the resulting volume.

Yes. And this actually exposes a major denominator problem in the token data we’ve been using.

Anthropic and OpenAI both now publish separate data for consumer/private usage versus enterprise/API usage. Once you separate them, your intuition is substantially right—but with an important twist: open models are disproportionately used by the most sophisticated/high-volume companies, not by the average business.

Anthropic’s Economic Index explicitly separates:

Claude.ai Free/Pro/Max → “consumer data”
Anthropic first-party API → “enterprise data”

In its January study, Anthropic sampled 1 million consumer conversations and 1 million first-party API interactions separately. Its newer June report continues breaking out Claude chat/Cowork and first-party API traffic separately. 

And the behavior is different. Anthropic says personal usage of Claude chat/Cowork is roughly 35% on weekdays and nearly 50% on weekends, while API traffic has a much lower personal-use baseline. The API side is dominated much more by automation and things like business communications, processing systems, classification and other repeated workflows. 

OpenAI does almost exactly the same thing now.

Its Signals Individual dataset covers Free/Go/Plus/Pro and explicitly excludes Enterprise and Codex; OpenAI warns that this therefore underrepresents business and technical usage. Separately, Enterprise Signals measures ChatGPT Enterprise, Codex and API intensity. 

And enterprise usage looks radically different. By June:

64% of combined ChatGPT + Codex enterprise output tokens came from Codex/agentic use.

The top 10% of enterprise customers by usage generated 8.3× as many output tokens per active employee as typical companies, versus only 2.6× in January. 

So we really have at least three different AI markets hiding inside the headline token numbers:

Market	Typical behavior
Consumer	“I like ChatGPT/Claude, so I pay $20–$30/month.” Very little model routing.
Normal enterprise	Buys ChatGPT/Claude seats/API because deployment, security, support and governance are easy.
AI-intensive enterprise / production app	Routes millions/billions of tokens and suddenly $0.20 vs $5 per million matters enormously. Uses multiple models and optimizes price/performance.

And the data supporting that third category is pretty striking.

Ramp basically tested your hypothesis

Ramp looked at actual corporate spending and used purchases from model-serving platforms as a proxy for companies accessing open-weight/Chinese models.

Only 5.8% of AI-spending businesses were using those platforms in June, up from 4.5% in January.

So open-source adoption is not yet widespread across ordinary corporations.

But look at who uses it:

Median AI spend/company using model-serving platforms: $248 per employee

versus

Median AI spend among all AI-spending companies: $10.59 per employee

That’s about 23× greater AI intensity. 

That’s almost exactly the population where a CFO/CTO starts caring about token economics.

Even more interesting: they aren’t abandoning OpenAI and Anthropic.

Among firms using model-serving platforms:

93.2% also use Anthropic
85.8% also use OpenAI
96.4% use at least one of them

So the emerging corporate architecture isn’t:

Anthropic → DeepSeek

It’s much more like:

Anthropic + OpenAI + DeepSeek/Qwen/GLM/etc., with workloads routed according to economics.

And this explains the bizarre Vercel numbers

Vercel isn’t measuring which chatbot humans prefer.

It’s measuring production applications routing API traffic.

That distinction is huge.

In June, only roughly one in eight Vercel enterprise customers was running an open-weight model in production.

Yet open weights already produced:

29% of ALL tokens

on

<4% of spending.

So a relatively small number of sophisticated customers can generate an enormous amount of open-model token volume.

And that phenomenon has since accelerated dramatically. The current Vercel leaderboard has DeepSeek alone around 42% of token volume while taking only about 4.6% of spending. 

That’s why we should be very careful saying:

“65% of tokens are open models, therefore 65% of AI users prefer open models.”

That would be completely wrong.

It could instead mean something like:

“A relatively small number of very high-volume production workloads have discovered that they don’t need a $10/$50 frontier model for every inference.”

And I think that’s actually the more economically important development.

There’s also a misconception about implementing open source

A corporation doesn’t necessarily need to download DeepSeek, hire ML engineers and build a GPU cluster.

They can consume open-weight models through managed inference providers/gateways almost exactly like they consume OpenAI:

Application → router → choose model → API call

The weights can ultimately run on somebody else’s infrastructure.

That’s why this can spread much faster among corporations than among ordinary consumers. The consumer sees:

ChatGPT app
Claude app
Gemini app

and picks one.

The enterprise sees:

Task A → premium model
Task B → cheap open model
Task C → small internal model
Task D → frontier reasoning model

with the employee potentially never knowing which model answered.

But here’s the counterintuitive part

Broad enterprise adoption is still heavily closed-model dominated.

Menlo’s 2025 enterprise survey actually found open-source LLM share falling from 19% to 11%, despite their rapidly improving capabilities. 

So I wouldn’t characterize it as:

Consumers = closed
Corporations = open

Yet.

I’d characterize it as:

Consumers → overwhelmingly branded closed-model interfaces

Ordinary corporations → overwhelmingly closed enterprise products because they’re easy, supported and governed

AI-native / very high-volume corporations → increasingly multi-model, with cheap/open models absorbing enormous low-value token volume

And that’s probably the most important segment for our commoditization thesis because those are the buyers that set the marginal economics of inference.

There is another useful implication: Vercel’s 65% open-token share and Anthropic’s 55% spend share can both be true without contradiction. The giant commodity workloads get dumped onto extremely cheap models while companies reserve Anthropic/OpenAI frontier models for the economically valuable tasks where an extra few dollars per million tokens are irrelevant compared with the value of getting the answer right.

That is almost exactly the barbell we’ve been looking for.

I think our weekly token report should now explicitly carry four separate datasets: consumer usage, enterprise seat/API usage, production-routing token share, and actual corporate spending. Otherwise we’re mixing fundamentally different buyers into one “AI demand” number.
