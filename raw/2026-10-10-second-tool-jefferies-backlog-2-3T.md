# Second-tool write-up: "Jefferies: AI's $2.3 trillion backlog problem" — Jake's paste, 2026-10-10 ~8:27pm PDT (verbatim, tables flattened)

The headline checks out on the size of the backlog. The claim that two AI labs account for roughly half is supported by Jefferies' estimates, but not independently confirmed by customer-level SEC disclosures. More importantly, this is exactly the counterparty concentration risk we've been tracing in the AI money map.

The $2.3 trillion is real. Latest reported figures: Microsoft $678B (June 2026) · Amazon $496B (June 2026) · Google Cloud $513.9B (June 2026) · Oracle $664B (August 2026) · Total $2.352T (mixed latest periods). Sources: company earnings disclosures and SEC filings. Amazon's figure covers longer-term contracts, while Microsoft's and Oracle's backlogs include businesses beyond AI cloud.
Jefferies previously estimated that OpenAI and Anthropic represented roughly half of the combined backlog. Its July estimates placed their shares at approximately 49% for Microsoft, 51% for Amazon, 43% for Google and 54% for Oracle.
Illustration: ~$1.17T estimated OpenAI + Anthropic exposure / ~$1.18T other customer backlog. Illustrative only: applying Jefferies' older customer-concentration percentages to today's reported balances. This is not a newly verified breakdown.
The question isn't whether those contracts were signed. It's whether the two labs can generate enough independent operating cash to fulfill them without repeatedly raising new capital.

What's new versus the July Jefferies warning? Some of the analysis appears to be recycled from Jefferies reports published in July and August. In August, Jefferies' updated estimates put the two labs at 30–40% of Microsoft's backlog, 40% of Google's and 45–50% of Amazon's—generally lower than the earlier figures. Oracle remains particularly opaque because it has not disclosed an equivalent customer-level breakdown.
There's another significant qualification: Anthropic reportedly achieved positive adjusted operating profit in consecutive 2026 quarters, excluding stock compensation. Its 2025 net loss was still enormous, and adjusted operating profit is not the same as positive free cash flow. But calling both labs simply unprofitable misses that change.

Why this resembles 2008—and where the analogy breaks: Investors and lenders supply capital to labs and infrastructure → OpenAI / Anthropic sign multiyear compute commitments → Cloud providers build against anticipated contract revenue → GPU suppliers / infrastructure lenders receive hardware payments or assume financing exposure; some investment capital flows back through the chain. A funding interruption could force a lab to reduce purchases or default on obligations. Cloud providers would then face weaker collections, lower equipment utilization, and potential impairments, while their own financing and infrastructure costs continue. But an RPO is not an outstanding loan. It's contracted future revenue contingent on service delivery and collection. Nor is all cloud financing dependent on those two labs. The closest 2008-style parallel is concentrated credit risk compounded by borrowing against expected future payments. The closest 2000-style parallel is building expensive technological infrastructure whose future economic value may disappoint.

The strongest new evidence is in the financing structures:
1. Broadcom's proposed $60B chip financing. Banks are arranging a $42B senior secured tranche with Broadcom residual-value support, plus $18B of junior debt led by Blackstone. The transaction has not been reported as fully completed.
2. Oracle's customer prepayments. Its August 10-Q discloses $11.4B of first-quarter customer prepayments with a significant financing component. This helps fund construction and reduces Oracle's need to borrow immediately, but it also creates obligations to deliver future services.
3. Credit availability is beginning to tighten. The Financial Times reported today that AI-related debt issuance dropped to $23B in September, less than half August's level.
Microsoft's FY2026 filing reports $24.1B in recognized OpenAI-related commercial revenue and $6B in OpenAI accounts receivable at June 30.

What I'd change in the money map: a fourth category: cash-flow-dependent contractual exposure, separate from realized revenue, outside-customer demand, and contingent credit guarantees. For each cloud provider, measure how much of the lab-linked backlog is scheduled to convert in the next 12, 24 and 36 months, versus how much cash the lab can generate or raise over those periods.
Bottom line: Jefferies' concern is substantiated, but the headline overstates what is known about the quality of that $1.17T. The more immediate risk may be in specialized GPU financing and leveraged cloud operators. Priority indicators: AI-related bond issuance, Oracle and CoreWeave credit spreads, and whether the Broadcom financing closes on its proposed terms.
