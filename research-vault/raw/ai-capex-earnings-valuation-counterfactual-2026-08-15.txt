S&P 500 AI-Capex Earnings & Valuation Report
Two complementary tests of whether the AI capital cycle is overstating earnings breadth and understating valuation risk
Scope
This report consolidates two separate analyses: (1) the current AI-stripped forward P/E test, and (2) a historical earnings-breadth counterfactual that asks what non-mega-cap S&P earnings might have looked like if the post-2021 AI capex cycle had not existed. The purpose is not to prove causation, but to test whether conventional breadth and headline P/E are giving a falsely reassuring picture.

Prepared from the analysis developed in this conversation. Current valuation figures refer to August 2026; historical GAAP earnings comparisons run through 2024. Dollar figures are approximate and rounded.

Executive Summary
The two tests point in the same direction, but they answer different questions. The forward P/E test asks how expensive the S&P 500 would look if earnings tied to the AI-capex chain were not capitalized as ordinary, permanent earnings power. The historical counterfactual asks whether the rest of the index has generated anything close to a normal earnings-growth path since the last pre-generative-AI baseline year.
Bottom line
The headline S&P 500 forward P/E near 20× can become roughly 24.7–25.2× when AI-capex-chain earnings are stripped from the denominator while the full index price is retained. Separately, aggregate GAAP earnings for the S&P 500 excluding Mag 7 + Oracle fell from about $1.342T in 2021 to about $1.277T in 2024, even while those eight companies’ earnings rose sharply. The combined implication is that headline valuation compression and earnings breadth may both be materially more dependent on one capital-spending cycle than conventional measures suggest.

1. Analytical Framework
Conventional market breadth counts securities, sectors, or moving-average participation. That can be misleading when one economic impulse—here, AI-related capital spending—transmits through many different industries. Semiconductors, servers, networking, power equipment, cooling, construction, materials, utilities, and financing can all appear as distinct winners while sharing the same underlying demand driver.
The report therefore separates two concepts:
Ticker breadth: how many stocks are rising or outperforming.
Economic-driver breadth: how many independent sources of earnings growth exist after stripping the AI-capex chain.
The two tests below approach the same issue from opposite directions: valuation today and earnings history since 2021.
2. Result One — AI-Stripped Forward P/E
2.1 Objective
The first test asks: if a meaningful portion of current forward S&P earnings is generated by the AI capital cycle, what multiple is the market actually paying on the earnings that remain after that cycle is stripped out?
The relevant formula is:
Cycle-adjusted valuation
Cycle-adjusted P/E = S&P 500 index price ÷ (reported forward S&P EPS − AI-capex-chain forward EPS). This is intentionally different from simply calculating the P/E of a non-AI portfolio. The full index price remains in the numerator because the test is asking how expensive today’s index price is relative to a normalized residual earnings base.

2.2 Baseline calculation
Using the August 7, 2026 FactSet snapshot, the S&P 500 was approximately 7,709.96 with a forward 12-month P/E of about 20.0×. That implies forward index EPS of roughly $385.5:
Implied EPS
7,709.96 ÷ 20.0 ≈ $385.5 of forward S&P 500 EPS.

A strict direct-supplier basket was used as a first pass. It included major semiconductor, memory, server, networking, storage, and semiconductor-equipment beneficiaries such as Nvidia, Broadcom, Micron, AMD, Intel, Lam Research, Applied Materials, Dell, KLA, Arista, Sandisk, Seagate, Amphenol, Marvell, and Western Digital. The basket represented roughly 17.7% of index market capitalization but about 19% of forward earnings in the rough construction because several of the largest names traded at relatively low forward multiples.

2.3 Why there are two different “ex-AI” multiples
There are two conceptually different calculations, and they should not be mixed:
Non-AI portfolio P/E: remove both the AI companies’ market capitalization and their earnings. This asks what the remaining stocks trade at. In the rough construction, this stayed near 20×.
Cycle-adjusted index P/E: retain the entire S&P price but strip AI-cycle earnings from the denominator. This asks how expensive the current index level is if those earnings are treated as cyclical or non-permanent. This rose to roughly 25×.
The second measure is the one relevant to the bubble/capital-cycle hypothesis. It is deliberately harsher because the market capitalization built on those earnings remains embedded in the index price.

2.5 The trend test that can falsify the thesis
The level test alone is not enough. The stronger claim is that the headline S&P P/E can compress while the underlying residual P/E actually rises because the denominator is being inflated by a capital-cycle earnings surge.
Between June 30 and August 7, 2026, the index price rose about 2.8% while forward EPS rose about 4.7%, causing the headline forward P/E to fall from roughly 20.4× to 20.0×. For residual P/E to have risen over that same interval, AI-capex-chain companies would have needed to account for roughly 52% or more of the total increase in forward S&P EPS estimates.
Falsification threshold
If AI-capex-chain companies accounted for less than roughly half of the forward-EPS estimate increase over that period, then residual EPS likely grew fast enough that the residual P/E also compressed. If they accounted for more than roughly half, headline multiple compression may be largely a denominator illusion.

This historical point-in-time estimate series has not yet been fully reconstructed. Therefore, the level result (~25× residual P/E) is stronger than the trend claim. The trend claim remains an empirical test, not a settled conclusion.
3. Result Two — Historical No-AI Earnings Counterfactual
3.1 Objective and baseline
The second test asks what ordinary S&P earnings breadth might have looked like if the AI-capex cycle had never become the dominant post-2021 investment impulse. The baseline strips Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia, Tesla, and Oracle from total S&P 500 aggregate GAAP earnings and then examines how the residual earnings base evolved.
For this purpose, 2021 is treated as the last reasonable pre-generative-AI anchor. The largest hyperscaler capex acceleration becomes more visible in 2023–2024, but using 2021 provides a clean before/after earnings base.

3.6 What this result does—and does not—show
The historical test does not show that AI pushed total S&P earnings above a normal trend. In fact, through 2024 the evidence points the other way: outside the eight largest technology/AI-adjacent companies, aggregate GAAP earnings were below their 2021 level and well below even conservative normalized growth paths.
That forces a revision of the strongest version of the original hypothesis. The more defensible interpretation is not “AI created excessive aggregate S&P earnings.” It is “AI masked unusually weak underlying earnings breadth and became the dominant source of net index earnings growth.”

4.1 The revised thesis
Revised working thesis
The AI capital cycle may be making the market look healthier in two ways at once: it inflates the earnings denominator used to justify the current index price, and it creates apparent breadth across many nominal sectors even when those sectors share the same underlying demand impulse. Meanwhile, the non-mega-cap earnings base has not demonstrated anything close to a normal post-2021 expansion on the historical GAAP evidence examined so far.

4.2 Why the residual P/E could be even higher
The historical analysis suggests that the ex-Mag7+Oracle earnings base was already weak before many second-order AI beneficiaries fully monetized the data-center buildout. If 2025–2026 forward earnings growth in Broadcom, Micron, Vertiv, Eaton, Quanta, power generation, construction, and financing is increasingly AI-dependent, then the current residual earnings denominator could be smaller than the rough ~19–20% strip used in the first valuation test.
If so, the earlier ~25× cycle-adjusted forward P/E may be a conservative estimate rather than an aggressive one. This is the central bridge between the two analyses.
5. What Would Falsify the Thesis?
A strong analytical framework should specify what evidence would prove it wrong. The most important falsification tests are:
Residual P/E compression: if the monthly AI-stripped P/E falls alongside the headline P/E, then the market is genuinely getting cheaper outside the AI cycle.
Broad residual earnings acceleration: if ex-AI earnings begin compounding at 6–10% or better without relying on data-center-linked demand, then the historical breadth weakness is healing.
Low attribution share: if detailed company disclosures show that AI/data-center revenue is only a small share of incremental EBIT across infrastructure, power, materials, and financials, then the economic-driver concentration thesis is overstated.
Independent sector leadership: if healthcare, consumer, industrial, energy, and financial profit growth accelerates for reasons unrelated to AI capex or AI financing, then conventional breadth becomes more trustworthy.
Forward-EPS revision test fails: if AI-capex names accounted for materially less than ~52% of the June 30–August 7 increase in forward S&P EPS estimates, then the claim that the residual multiple rose during headline compression weakens.
6. Important Limitations
GAAP vs. forward earnings: the historical counterfactual uses aggregate GAAP profit dollars, while the valuation test uses forward 12-month EPS estimates. They are complementary but not directly interchangeable.
Fiscal-year mismatch: Apple, Nvidia, Oracle, Microsoft, and others have fiscal calendars that do not perfectly align with S&P calendar-year aggregate earnings.
Index composition changes: “the S&P 500” is not a fixed set of companies through time. A fully rigorous study would use point-in-time constituents and float-adjusted weights.
Attribution uncertainty: Eaton, Dell, Quanta, utilities, banks, and similar companies have substantial non-AI businesses. Removing all of their earnings would overstate the AI strip; removing none would understate it.
2021 is an imperfect baseline: it was an unusually strong reopening year. That is why the analysis emphasizes normalized 6%, 8%, and 10% growth paths rather than relying solely on the 13.44% 2016–2021 CAGR.
Causality is not established: earnings weakness outside the AI complex can reflect rates, commodity cycles, post-pandemic normalization, taxes, inflation, or other macro forces. The test identifies concentration and divergence, not a single causal mechanism.
Financing effects remain under-modeled: banks, private credit, asset managers, insurers, and capital-markets firms can benefit from AI-related borrowing and project finance without being obvious AI companies. That channel has not yet been fully quantified.
7. Highest-Value Next Step
The next calculation should merge the two approaches into a point-in-time forward earnings series from 2021 through 2026. For each month or quarter, compute reported S&P forward EPS, ex-Mag7+Oracle forward EPS, and an attributable AI-capex-adjusted forward EPS series. Then compare each series with the index price.
The ideal output would contain three valuation lines:
Headline S&P forward P/E.
S&P forward P/E after removing Mag7 + Oracle earnings.
Full AI-capex-chain-adjusted P/E after removing attributable supplier, infrastructure, power, construction, and financing earnings.
The most important question is whether the third line is rising even when the first line is flat or falling. If that occurs, it would be direct evidence that apparent valuation compression is being created by capital-cycle earnings rather than by genuinely cheaper underlying market earnings.

9. One-Paragraph Conclusion
The combined evidence says that the current S&P 500 may be less broadly healthy than headline breadth and valuation measures imply. The forward valuation test suggests that stripping AI-capex-chain earnings can raise the effective multiple from roughly 20× to about 25×. The historical counterfactual shows that the rest of the S&P excluding Mag7 + Oracle had lower aggregate GAAP earnings in 2024 than in 2021 and sat hundreds of billions of dollars below plausible normalized growth paths. That does not prove the entire market is an AI bubble, nor does it prove every post-2021 earnings gain is AI-driven. It does show that one unusually large capital-spending cycle may be doing disproportionate work in both the earnings denominator and the apparent breadth of winners. The decisive next test is a point-in-time 2021–2026 residual forward-EPS series: if the residual P/E rises while headline P/E falls, the capital-cycle explanation becomes much harder to dismiss.
